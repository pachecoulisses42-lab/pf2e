# Dado Sólido — Solid Die

*A visual philosophy of weight, outline and consequence.*

Dado Sólido treats every mark as a physical object that has been set down on a table. Forms are cut from flat, saturated colour and fenced by a single heavy black contour; behind each one a hard, unblurred shadow is thrown a fixed distance down and to the right, as if the shape were a thick card lifted a few millimetres off the page. There is no gradient, no glow, no atmosphere. What you see is exactly what is there, and that honesty is the entire aesthetic.

Space is organised by a quiet geometry: the regular polygon, the circle, the single diagonal. Each composition holds one subject at monumental scale and gives it room to breathe inside a generous safe zone, so the silhouette reads in a fraction of a second at a glance and survives any mask that is laid over it. Hierarchy is created by contrast of mass, not by detail: one bold figure, one answering accent, nothing that does not earn its place.

Colour is a small, fixed chorus. Mustard, coral, green, steel blue and violet each sit on a ground of cream or in a field of their own, and black is the single constant that binds them. Chosen once and held with discipline, the palette behaves like a printer's spot-colour set: every hue pure, every junction between two hues sharpened by the black line that separates them.

Typography, where it appears, is a single heavy geometric face used as a shape rather than a caption: a numeral struck once, large, with the same outline and shadow as the forms around it. Words are replaced by marks wherever a mark can carry the meaning.

Every contour must be drawn as though by a master of the craft: stroke weights identical across the piece, corners resolved with deliberate rounding, offsets measured to the pixel, nothing nudged by eye. The finished object should look meticulously constructed over countless hours of refinement, the product of deep expertise and painstaking attention, with the calm authority of work that has nothing left to remove.
